# pipboy-map — Design Context

## Users

**Primary:** RPG players browsing fantasy worlds *between* sessions. At home, on a laptop, no time pressure. The map is reference material for an active campaign or personal lore appreciation — not a tool used at the table during play.

**Posture:** Relaxed, exploratory, willing to linger for atmosphere. They are emphatically *not* trying to look up an address fast. They want to wander.

**Job to be done:** "Let me feel like I have a working artifact from inside the fiction, and let me use it to revisit a world I love."

This audience changes priorities versus a GM-at-the-table tool: scan-speed and chrome density matter less; immersion, rhythm, and tactile detail matter more.

## Brand Personality

**Three words:** salvaged, stubborn, opinionated.

**Voice:** Lived-in salvage tech. Warm under the grime. Decades of fingerprints. Things have hand-typed labels stuck on them and some labels are crooked. The device has been carried, dropped, repaired, and re-labelled by people who needed it to work, not for people who needed it to look new.

**Emotional goal:** The user opens the app and forgets Angular exists for a few minutes. The interface earns its place by acting as a prop in their RPG headcanon, not as software wrapped around a map.

**Microcopy register:** Etched, stencilled, type-written. `ALL MAPS` is fine. `Browse all worlds →` is wrong. Status lines (`STANDBY`, `LOCKED`, `OFFLINE`) are part of the prop. Hand-numbered IDs, signal-strength bars, version strings, dates in YYYY.MM format — these read as machine output from inside the world.

## Aesthetic Direction

**Theme:** Dark, locked. Green phosphor on near-black is the medium. Not negotiable.

**References:**
- The actual Pip-Boy 3000 (Fallout 3 / 4 / 76) — chunky bezels, hand-stencilled labels, mechanical dial, lived-in casing.
- CRT terminals: DEC VT100, IBM 3270 monochrome, oscilloscope screens.
- Survival-manual print: dot-matrix typesetting, pre-printed forms, faded carbon copies, rubber-stamp dates.
- Vault-Tec instructional posters: stencil + thick rule + ironic reassurance.
- Field-equipment labelling: Dymo embossing tape, gaffer-tape scrawl, rivets and screw heads as honest hardware.

**Anti-references** (what this must NOT look like):
- The sibling LCARS variants in `front/lcars-map*` — those exist for Trek; this is a different costume.
- The cyberpunk upstream (`cbrpnk-map`) — magenta/cyan neon belongs to that fork, not this one.
- Generic Stripe/Linear/Vercel "AI dashboard" — soft shadows, rounded cards, gradient text, glassmorphism. Every one of those is forbidden here.
- Modern minimal map UIs (Mapbox, Google Maps satellite chrome) — pristine, sterile, brand-neutral.

**Theme detail:** The CRT is alive. Subtle scanlines (already wired through `CbrpnkService`), gentle phosphor bloom on bright edges, a faint horizontal jitter on warm-up, vignette at the corners. These earn their place when the user can describe what they add — they are *never* decorative noise.

## Design Principles

1. **It's a prop, not a product.** The UI is an object found inside the fiction. Every label, button, and status line should plausibly have been printed, etched, or stuck on by someone in the world. "AI design language" reflexes (cards-with-icon-and-heading, pill badges, soft hover states, ghost buttons with arrow icons) all break the spell — refuse them by default.

2. **Phosphor green is the only chroma.** Hierarchy comes from luminance, weight, density, and rule-work — not from a second accent hue. The one exception is a single warning amber for genuine alarm states; it must remain rare or it stops working.

3. **Texture earns its place.** Scanlines, bloom, jitter, vignette, paper grain, stencilled rules: each one must answer "what does this add?" in plain words. If the answer is "atmosphere," look harder; atmosphere is the result of correct decisions, not the decisions themselves.

4. **Monospace rhythm beats size jumps.** This is a fixed-pitch terminal. Build hierarchy through column width, line-height, ASCII rules (`────`, `▓▒░`, `[ ]`), labelling conventions (`>>>`, `001/847`), and weight — not through oversized display type. Type sizes stay close together; the scale is narrow on purpose.

5. **Slow over fast.** Browsing audience, not transacting audience. Loaders can take a beat (CRT warm-up, channel-tuning rasterisation). Hovers can reveal slowly. The timeline scrubber should feel mechanical — frame-stepped, not fluid. Snappy SaaS feedback is wrong here.

6. **Lived-in beats clean.** A handful of intentional imperfections — a slightly crooked label, a "PROPERTY OF…" stamp at the footer, a registration mark misaligned by 1px, a smudge in the corner — sell the prop. They must look authored, not random; the design pays for them with the precision of everything else.
